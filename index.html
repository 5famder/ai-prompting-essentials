Lesson 1.2 · 4 min · Supportive information

Why vague prompts fail

An AI assistant writes the most likely answer to what you asked. When you leave something out, it fills the gap with the average.

HeyGen video · 1:16
M
It fills the gap with the average.
Transcript

You ask an AI assistant to write an email. It gives you something polite, correct… and useless. You rewrite half of it yourself. Today we'll see why that happens, and it's not because the tool is bad.

Here's the prompt most of us write. I ran it three times. Notice anything? Every version is fine. None of them is yours. None mentions the real reason, the new date, or who's reading.

An AI assistant doesn't know your project. It writes the most likely answer to what you asked. When you leave something out, it fills the gap with the average: the email that fits every project, which means it fits none.

So a vague prompt isn't a small problem. Every gap you leave is a decision the model makes for you, and it will always choose the safest, blandest option.

Now watch what happens when we fill those gaps. The task: tell the client the launch moves by two weeks. The context: a supplier delay, and a client who values honesty. The constraints: under 120 words, calm tone. The format: a subject line plus three short paragraphs.

Same tool, same model. The difference is the brief. And that's the key idea for this whole course: a prompt is a brief. If a new colleague couldn't do the task from your message, neither can the model.

In the next lesson you'll get the four elements as a checklist you can keep. Then it's your turn.

Four elements turn a request into a brief. You don't need all four every time, but check for each one before you send.

Lesson 2.1 · 3 min · Supportive information

Show, don't tell

An adjective like "professional" means something different to everyone. An example means one thing.

HeyGen video · 1:20
M
An adjective can't tell the difference.
Transcript

Yesterday you learned that a prompt is a brief. Today we'll add the most powerful thing you can put in a brief: your own material.

Ask for a "professional" email and you'll get the model's idea of professional: formal, long, a little stiff. Your idea might be warm and short. An adjective can't tell the difference. An example can.

Instead of describing the style, show it. Paste a message you've already sent, and say: match this tone. One or two good examples do more than a paragraph of adjectives.

Your material doesn't have to be an example of style. It can be the notes you want summarised, a report, or even a photo of a whiteboard. Most assistants accept all three.

Two habits make material work. First, label it: tell the model what it is, and mark where it starts and ends. Three quotation marks work fine. Second, say what to do with it: summarise it, copy its structure, or match its tone.

And one check before you paste: documents are full of names and numbers. Remove what the model doesn't need. You learned how yesterday.

Let's see it on a real example: messy meeting notes, and a manager with two minutes to read them.

Key ideaShow, don't tell. One good example does more than a paragraph of adjectives.

Lesson 3.1 · 3 min · Supportive information

Diagnose before you retry

An AI assistant sounds just as sure when it's wrong as when it's right. When an answer misses, find the cause before you change anything.

HeyGen video · 1:28
M
Confident is not the same as correct.
Transcript

Yesterday you saw the AI copy a format perfectly and still get the facts wrong. Today is about what to do when the answer is wrong.

An AI assistant sounds just as sure when it's wrong as when it's right. It added a currency to our budget, then changed it. It invented a date. None of it was flagged. Confident is not the same as correct.

When an answer misses, there's usually one of four causes. The model didn't have the facts. The limits were vague. It didn't know what good looks like. Or you asked for too much at once.

Each cause has its own fix. Add the facts. Name the limit: length, tone, audience. Show an example. Or split the task into steps. Diagnose first, then fix. Just pressing "regenerate" is a lottery.

Before you use any answer, run a three-question check. Is every fact in my material, or in a source I trust? Are names, numbers and dates exactly right? And would I sign my name under it?

One more thing to check: bias. Ask for a job ad for a "strong, young team player", and the model will describe exactly that person, and leave everyone else out. The words in your prompt shape who appears in the answer.

Follow up or start fresh

Once you know the cause, decide where to fix it: in the same chat, or in a new one.

Follow up in the same chatwhen the answer is close and you want a small change: shorter, warmer, add a greeting. The model keeps what you already told it.
Start a new chatwhen the answer went off track, when the chat is full of earlier attempts, or when you pasted something sensitive earlier.
Don't just regeneratewithout changing the prompt. You'll get a different answer, not a better one.

The leading question

If your question assumes something false, the model usually goes along with it. Read this answer. Does anything look wrong?

PROMPTExplain to the team why the 3 November launch was cancelled, based on the meeting notes below.

Meeting notes:
"""
Weekly sync 14 Oct. … Launch 3 Nov still realistic if staging is fixed by Monday. Developer to report Monday. Next sync 21 Oct.
"""

The 3 November launch was cancelled because the staging server instability put the testing timeline at risk.

At the 14 October sync, the launch was still considered realistic, but only if the staging environment was fixed by the following Monday. Since the server had already gone down twice that week, the team could not rely on having a stable environment for testing. Other work was also incomplete—only 12 of 20 product pages were written, and the homepage still needed final copy—but the critical issue identified in the notes was the unresolved staging problem.

So the key message to the team is: the launch was cancelled because the technical testing timeline could not be secured, making a 3 November release no longer reliable.

Lesson 4.1 · 3 min · Supportive information

Break it down

Some jobs are too big for one prompt. Split them into steps, and ask for answers in a shape you can use straight away.

HeyGen video · 2:20
M
One job, one prompt.
Transcript

Some jobs are too big for one prompt. Today you'll learn to split them into steps, and to get answers in a shape you can use straight away.

One job, one prompt. Ask for an agenda, a materials list, a budget and an invitation all at once, and the model does each of them a little. Something gets skipped, something gets rushed, and you can't tell which part went wrong.

Instead, build a chain. Step one: the agenda. Step two: the materials list, based on that agenda. Step three: the invitation, based on both. Each step uses the one before it.

Stay in the same chat, so the model remembers each step. And check between steps: a mistake in step one travels all the way to step three.

Ask for the shape you need. An agenda works best as a table: time, session, goal. Materials as a checklist. Name the columns, and the model will fill them.

So: one job per prompt, a check after each step, and a clear shape for every answer. That's how long tasks stay under control.

Lesson 4.2 · 3 min · Procedural information

How to write a chain

Three moves. You'll see all three in the worked example next.

Listen instead1:03 · narrated by the course designer's AI voice
Same content as the text below. Read or listen, whichever suits you.
1Start with the facts, then one job. Give the context once, in the first message, and end with “First, … only.” The word only stops the model running ahead.
2Name the shape. Not “an agenda” but “a table with three columns: time, session, goal”. Not “the materials” but “a checklist grouped by session”.
3Link each step to the last. Start with “Now…” and point back: “based on the agenda above”. The model builds on what you already checked.

Which shape?

SHAPEUSE IT FORSAY
TableAgendas, comparisons, plans with owners“as a table with columns: …”
ChecklistMaterials, steps someone will follow“as a checklist grouped by …”
Short listIdeas, options, names“give me 5 options, one line each”
When is one prompt enough?When the answer is one piece you can check in one read: a reply, a summary, a rewrite. When the job has parts that depend on each other, use a chain.

Lesson 5.1 · 2 min · Supportive information

Make it reusable

A good prompt you write once and use every week is worth more than a perfect prompt you write once.

HeyGen video · 1:30
M
Fix the template, not just the answer.
Transcript

Welcome to the last day. You've learned to write a clear request, to work from your own material, to check the answer, and to break big jobs into steps. Today you'll turn all of that into something you keep: a prompt template.

A template is a prompt you write once and reuse every week. The parts that stay the same are written out: the structure, the length, the tone, the rules. The parts that change are placeholders in square brackets: the audience, this week's notes.

Add one line that protects you: "Use only facts from the notes. If something is unclear, list it under To check instead of guessing." It helps. It doesn't do your checking for you.

Before you trust a template, test it on two different real cases. If it works on both, keep it. If it doesn't, fix the template, not just the answer.
